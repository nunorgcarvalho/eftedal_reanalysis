\section{Revising Eftedal et al.'s results}

\begin{frame}{How Eftedal et al.\ fit their model}{(1/2)}
  \begin{fittable}
  Identified 82 relationship types, split by type of relative, sex of the pair, and the side of the
  family through which the pair is related.
  & Distant relatives have a greater number of ``distinct'' relationship types.
  & Grouped the relationship types into 10 broad types of relatives. \\ \rowgap
  Minimized the residual sum of squares (RSS; squared difference between predicted and observed
  correlation, summed across relationship types).
  & Distant relative types (with more ``distinct'' relationships) make up a greater share of the RSS.
  & No change. \\ \rowgap
  Optimized for log-transformed correlations, which makes optimization a simpler linear regression
  problem.
  & Squared errors are greater for smaller raw correlations, so distant relatives make up an
  \emph{even greater} share of the RSS.
  & Optimized for observed correlations on the raw scale, using non-linear optimization. \\ \rowgap
  Weighted each relationship type's residual by the inverse variance of its observed correlation.
  & Effectively optimizes for the marginal pair of individuals in the full population, which is
  overwhelmingly weighted towards distant relatives (much more numerous, with lower observed
  correlations). Distant relatives make up an \emph{even greater} share of the RSS.
  & Fit for the Marginal Type instead: each of the 10 relative types gets equal total weight; within a
  type, relationship types are weighted by inverse variance. \\
  \end{fittable}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How Eftedal et al.\ fit their model}{(2/2)}
  \begin{fittable}
  Inverse-variance weights based on the raw correlation ($1/\mathrm{SE}^2$), not the log-correlation
  ($\approx \rho^2/\mathrm{SE}^2$).
  & Distant relatives (who have low correlations) make up an \emph{even greater} share of the RSS.
  & Confirmed that the correct inverse-variance weights on the log scale give estimates similar to
  optimizing on the raw scale. \\ \rowgap
  Used the wrong expected correlation for step-siblings, $V_A\,m\,\big((1+m)/2\big)^2$. The
  primary assortative mating model they assume throughout instead predicts
  $V_A\,m\,\big((1+r)/2\big)^2$.
  & Predicts step-siblings to be less similar than the model actually implies (since $r > m$).
  & Used the correct formula. \\ \rowgap
  Estimated $m$ and $r$ independently, despite the primary assortative mating model implying
  $m = V_A\,r$.
  & Mismatch between the estimator and the estimand.
  & Enforced the constraint implied by the model. \\ \rowgap
  Confidence intervals from bootstrap re-sampling of the observed correlations (relationship types drawn
  with probability $\propto 1/\mathrm{SE}^2$), using the same transformation and weights as the point
  estimates.
  & The same biases as the point estimates (fits overwhelmingly to distant relatives), and overly
  confident intervals.
  & Confidence intervals from a leave-one-type-out jackknife over the 10 relative types. \\
  \end{fittable}
\end{frame}
